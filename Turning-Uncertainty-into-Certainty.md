# Turning Uncertainty into Certainty: The Fourth Stage of Working with AI

Author: @v5ria WK

> [中文](./把不确定变成确定——AI的第四个阶段.md) · English

Let me start with a morning. It's an illustration, not something that has already happened.

On Sunday night, just before bed, you mention in passing that you're meeting a client next week. On Monday morning the AI has already pulled together what you need on that client: a year of email threads, the two open questions left in the minutes of your last meeting, and the announcements they've made recently. The meeting outline is drafted, with pricing at the end, because last time you said you didn't want to open with money. It has also found three slots that are free on both calendars and is waiting for you to pick one.

That same morning, last week's numbers are already summarised in your inbox, and a draft of your weekly report is ready.

You didn't ask for any of this, and it didn't ask you. But every item is something you would have asked for.

That isn't how things work today. Today you have to know what you want and say it clearly before the AI does anything. This essay tries to work through three questions. What stands between today and that morning? What foundations have to be built before we get there? And what is worth preparing now?

## Four stages

I find it useful to split the division of labour between people and AI into four stages.

In stage one, you say one thing and it does one thing. "Reply to this email." "Turn this meeting recording into minutes." However fast it is, you still push every step.

In stage two, you set a goal and it runs to the end. Before leaving work you say, "Finish the research on these three competitors and have it ready tomorrow morning." It works for hours, gathering sources, comparing prices, and writing conclusions, and by the morning it's mostly done.

In stage three, you assign an AI supervisor to a team of AIs. One AI gathers material, one writes, one builds the slides, and the supervisor hands out the work, checks it, and sends it back when it's wrong. You still set the goal, but directing and checking no longer need you. This is where we are now.

In stage four, the AI turns uncertainty into certainty on its own. You have only a vague thought, such as "I'm seeing a client next week," or no thought at all yet. The AI works out what you want from your habits and rules, sets the goal and the bar itself, does the work, and puts the result in front of you.

| Stage | What you do | What the AI does |
|---|---|---|
| 1. You say one thing, it does one thing | Give every instruction | Follows it |
| 2. You set a goal, it runs to the end | Set one clear goal | Works for hours and finishes |
| 3. You assign an AI supervisor | You still set the goal | The supervisor directs and checks a team of AIs |
| 4. The AI turns uncertainty into certainty | Make the call, guard against harm, take the return | Works out what you want, sets the goal and the bar, gets it done |

The first three stages look very different, but one thing stays the same across all of them. Turning a vague idea into a clear task is always the person's job. For "meeting a client," you have to decide what to prepare, how deep the research should go, and what the outline should look like before the AI can start. The AI keeps getting more capable, and that step keeps staying with you.

In stage four, that step moves to the AI.

## What stage four adds

"Turning uncertainty into certainty" breaks down into three steps.

First, it works out what should be done. "I'm seeing a client next week" says nothing about pulling email history, putting pricing last, or booking a time. The AI infers those unspoken parts from how you've prepared for meetings before and how you've edited its outlines. The biggest rule is usually that the return has to exceed the cost. Half a day preparing for a small client isn't worth it; half a day for a major one is.

Second, it decides what "done well" means. It sets the evaluation criteria: how complete the background needs to be, how long the outline should run, which issue must be on the agenda.

Third, it finishes the work and puts it in front of you. What you get is an outline you can use as is and three times to choose from, not a starting point you still have to fill in. Usually it adds a better alternative, such as "this client might be better served by a phone call first."

What's left for the person is clear. The AI is responsible for turning the vague into the definite. You are responsible for making the call, holding the one line that it must not harm you, and taking the return.

## What makes a piece of work count

Before stage four is possible, there is a hurdle to clear: when an AI does something on your behalf, what makes it count?

Today the answer is that you were there. You looked at it and clicked confirm, and only then did the email go out or the expense claim get filed. In my own work that answer is getting harder to keep up. The AI is getting faster, and something it finishes overnight can take me a whole morning to read. More and more often I just skim it and click confirm. I clicked, but I didn't really check.

Follow that direction far enough and, at some point, the rule that everything must be seen by a person before it counts stops holding up on its own. Nobody has to abolish it. The shift I project for 2027 is this: whether something counts will no longer depend on whether you were present. It will depend on three things you set up in advance.

The first is acceptance criteria. You write them ahead of time, and the AI Agent doing the work can neither see them nor change them. The second is authorisation: what it may do and how much it may spend, verifiable by the other side and revocable by you at any moment. The third is a record that can only be appended to, never edited, including by any AI Agent that passes the work along.

I run a short-drama business, and the shift may show up there sooner. Here is an example with made-up numbers. One morning you see a message: 14 orders came in last night, 13 were delivered, and 1 was stopped by your acceptance criteria and refunded automatically. Every order was placed by someone else's AI Agent, and those AI Agents checked only three things. Whose criteria was the episode accepted against? Was your AI Agent authorised to take payment? Could the process record be verified? With all three in place, they paid.

So who ends up as the owner in this round? Not the person with the most AI Agents, because anyone can buy AI Agents. What can't be bought are three things. One is a set of acceptance criteria checked again and again against real results. Another is a record built up day by day from now on, which can't be written after the fact. The last is an authorisation you can withdraw whenever you choose. Whoever holds those three doesn't need to be present, but carries the liability for every piece of work and keeps the return.

## Wrong guesses and spending are not the real risk

There are three common worries about stage four. It might guess wrong. It might spend money it shouldn't. It might harm you.

I don't think the first two are serious. AI has already made trying things very cheap. It doesn't need to guess right the first time; it can hand you three versions of the outline and you pick one. Spending works the same way. It can tell which costs need your sign-off and which don't. It files a small taxi receipt on its own, but asks before buying a year's subscription to a paid database.

The one thing that has to be guarded is the third: it must not harm you. The difficulty is that "do no harm" is itself one of your unspoken rules. The AI won't know it on its own, so it has to be written down from things you have turned down before. That means which emails it may never send for you, which meetings it may never accept for you, which clients it must not contact first, and above what single amount it must ask you.

## What has to be built before stage four

This is the part I've thought about most.

Put the question another way. For an AI supervisor to set goals and standards on your behalf without causing serious damage, what does it need to be able to answer before every action? I come up with six questions. What do you want? What must it not do? Is the work good? What can it use? What happens if it gets something wrong? Are you satisfied? Each question needs a foundation behind it.

| Foundation | Question it answers | What's missing today |
|---|---|---|
| Record | What do you want? | Your edits and rejections aren't kept |
| Rules and limits | What must it not do? | Your limits mostly live in your head |
| Evaluation | Is the work good? | The AI's scores have never been checked against yours |
| Permissions and resources | What can it use? | Either nothing, or your own login, which can't be taken back |
| A holding area | What if it's wrong? | Work goes straight out with no step in between |
| Delivery and feedback | Are you satisfied? | Your yes or no doesn't become a new record |

**The record.** This is your past judgement: what you changed, what you rejected, and why you let something through. Stage four has to infer rules you never stated, and this record is the only material it can infer them from. In office work it's the email where you changed "Hello" to "Dear Mr Wang," the weekly report where you cut the adjectives, and the deck you sent back with "too long." Today most of those edits disappear once they're made, and next time the AI starts guessing from scratch. What's missing is a place that keeps each edit, and the reason you gave, in time order, where nothing can be changed afterwards.

**Rules and limits.** This is a written list of what counts as harm, a spending cap, and a concrete way to work out whether the return exceeds the cost. Without this layer, an AI that sets its own goals can set goals you don't want. In office work it might be a few lines. Outgoing email is drafted, never sent. It never accepts a meeting invitation for you. Any single expense above an amount you set needs your approval. The time and money spent on a task should match what the task is worth to you. Today these mostly live in your head, or in the instructions for one conversation, and they're gone when the conversation ends. What's missing is a fixed, versioned list that the AI checks before every payment and every outgoing message.

**Evaluation.** This is the standard the AI uses to score its own output. In stage four the AI decides what "done well" means. If its standard differs from yours, it will very efficiently produce things you don't want. In office work, the question is whether the minutes it rates as good are minutes you'd rate as good. AI already grades itself today, but few people compare its scores with their own judgement, item by item. What's missing is that comparison, done continuously. Each week, pick a few things it scored highly, judge them yourself, and use the mismatches to correct its standard. The AI doing the work must not be able to change that standard.

**Permissions and resources.** These are the tools, data, accounts, and money the AI can use. To finish a job it has to be able to read your calendar and email, book a room, and pay small amounts. Today the common setups sit at two extremes. Either it gets nothing and the work stalls halfway, or it uses your own login, so you can't tell what it did and can't easily take access back. What's missing is a separate identity and authorisation for the AI, with a written scope and an expiry, that you can revoke in one step.

**A holding area.** This is where finished work waits for your decision. In stage four the AI will do many things you didn't ask for, and some of them will be wrong guesses. That's fine, as long as the wrong guesses don't go straight out. In office work it means the email stays in drafts, the meeting invitation stays pending, and the slides stay in a folder. Today a lot of automation sends things out the moment they're done, with no step in between.

**Delivery and feedback.** This decides whether you can see at a glance, first thing in the morning, what it did and why. It also decides whether your "yes" or "no" turns back into a new record. Without this layer, none of the other five get any more accurate. In office work it's a one-page list each morning: what it did, why it did each thing, what it spent, and what's waiting for your decision. Today you have to click through several apps to find out what the AI did overnight. And when you say no, it doesn't learn what to do differently next time.

These six don't have to be built all at once. The record and the limits come first, because they can only be built up from today forward, and without them the other layers have nothing to stand on.

## Why start now

The part of this that takes the most time is the AI's own learning. It has to learn three things.

First, it has to learn you. Whether an email is ready to send, or a proposal is ready for your boss, depends on habits you can't quite put into words. None of this comes with a manual. An AI Agent can only pick it up one piece of work at a time, by watching what you change, what you reject, and why you approve. The record layer above is its learning material.

Second, it has to learn from experts. How finance reads an expense claim, how legal reads a contract, how sales decides whether a lead is worth chasing: the most valuable judgement sits with experienced people. Each time the AI Agent produces a version and an expert points out what's wrong, it records one more lesson and makes that mistake less often.

Third, it has to learn to find the best answer by itself. Only with your criteria and the experts' feedback can it produce dozens of versions, score them, discard most, and keep the best.

A person who learns for a year gains a year of experience. An AI Agent that learns for a year gains tens of thousands of attempts and pieces of feedback in the same time. Someone who starts early will, in a few years, have an AI Agent that understands them and the work and can pick the best answer itself. Someone who starts late gets an AI Agent that has just been installed and hasn't learned anything yet.

Preparing now looks early, because most of the time today's AI still needs to be told clearly before it acts. But a record can only be kept from today forward. The edits you made and the reasons behind them in the past are already gone.

## What to prepare now

If you use AI for office work, my suggestion is to pick one kind of task first, such as replying to email. Starting today:

- Record every edit you make to its drafts, and why.
- Write a list of limits no longer than one page.
- Let it draft but never send.
- Each week, compare its own scores on a few emails with your judgement of them.

Once that kind of task works, add the next.

My own preparation is in the short-drama business, and it maps onto the same layers.

- **The record:** a work log for every episode, from goal and shots to review results. Alongside it, a failure record: which kinds of visual inconsistency show up under which conditions, and which changes raised one score while making the episode as a whole worse.
- **Evaluation:** a scoring standard for video. It checks whether people, clothing, props, actions, and settings stay consistent across multiple 10-to-15-second generated clips, with a fixed version the working AI Agent can't change.
- **The limits:** versioned rule files stating which genres we don't touch and which ad channels we don't use.
- **Permissions:** a separate authorisation for one AI Agent and one small payment, confirmed to be revocable within a minute.
- **The holding area:** at first I open just one service, and only my own test AI Agent can call it.

For the order of work, I've given myself 90 days to run a single project and complete five things in sequence:

1. a fixed scoring standard;
2. a complete record that traces every action back to the original goal;
3. a set of trial records that counts failures too;
4. one revoked authorisation;
5. at most one service, open only to my own test AI Agent.

If the first three aren't done, the last two don't expand.

## Where we might be wrong

This projection could be wrong. What worries me most is that progress slows down. AI capability may not keep climbing the way it has; it may stall somewhere, or be held back by computing power or electricity. If so, stage four will arrive much later than I expect.

So that I'm not judging by feel, I've set five signals with a deadline of 31 December 2027.

1. **Acceptance.** In public measurements, do the tasks models can finish with 80% reliability exceed roughly 4 to 8 hours of human work? And does at least one frontier lab or mature open-source project accept multi-day AI Agent output through automated checks by default?
2. **Authorisation.** Does at least one mainstream payment or e-commerce channel require AI Agents to present authorisation separate from a login account when paying or ordering, as a default condition?
3. **Payment.** Are there countable AI Agent payments made with no person present? Or have unauthorised AI Agent payments produced chargeback rules, insurance, or dispute cases?
4. **Layers.** Do mainstream frameworks let rules govern the lowest-level actions directly? Or do structures with three or more layers of AI Agents consistently lose to a single strong AI Agent directing the work?
5. **The overall test.** At the deadline, check three things: do outward-facing actions still need a person's live approval, does a login account still count as authorisation, and are long tasks still accepted by a person reading everything? If all three still hold, this judgement was wrong. I stop expanding the preparation and keep only the simplest formats.

Even if I'm wrong, or it comes slowly, the record, the list of limits, and a scoring standard checked against your own judgement are all useful in today's work. If you start building them now, the worst outcome is that you started a little early.
